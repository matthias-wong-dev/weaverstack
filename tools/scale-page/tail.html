Real estate from ilovegov.datawithoutguessing.com, built and operated with Weaver. Item names are that project's own. Scroll the graph to move around it, and select a node to trace its lineage. Weaver's own catalogue tables are left out, because they say nothing about the size of the estate.

What the picture is showing you

One graph, not two

The Lakehouse and the Warehouse are separate Fabric items with separate engines. The dashed lines running between them are shortcuts, and they are dependencies like any other. Load walks the whole thing in one order.

Layers came out of the code

Nobody assigned those ten layers. They fell out of the Python imports and the relations the T-SQL selects from. Add a join to a query and the layer changes on the next build.

Files are first-class

The green nodes on the left of the Lakehouse are folders: Python programs that fetch spreadsheets, JSON and PDFs from real sources. They are graph nodes, so the tables downstream of them wait for them.

Checks hang off the estate

The violet nodes are Tests and Assumptions, installed alongside the objects they read. Their results go into the Catalogue, so health can answer questions about them later.

This is also the graph Weaver plans against. When you rebuild one object, its descendants are the ones that stop being certified. When you ask for health, "is this table behind its sources" is answered by walking the ancestry above. Selecting a node above will show you how far that reaches.

Performance benchmarks coming

We have not published performance numbers yet, and we are not going to make any up. Large-estate performance, no-op build and incremental-change benchmarks are being prepared and will be published here when they mean something.

When they arrive, they will be measuring the things that actually matter at this size:

  • Work avoided. How long a build takes when nothing has changed, which for most days is the real number.
  • Small-change rebuilds. One object edited in an estate of a thousand, and how much gets touched.
  • Descendant selection. How precisely a change is scoped to what it actually affects.
  • Structural churn avoided. How much of the physical estate a build leaves alone.
  • Recovery. What the second run costs after a failed first one.
  • Large incremental loads, and how they scale with the size of the change rather than the size of the table.

Rows per second will not be the headline. It is the least interesting number a mature pipeline produces, because the fastest way to process a row is not to have to.
